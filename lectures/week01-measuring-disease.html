At a glance

Epidemiology starts with counting: how many people are affected, how fast new cases appear, and how much more likely an exposed group is to be affected than an unexposed one. Five ideas carry the rest of the course: frequency measures, incidence vs. prevalence, measures of association, standardization, and screening test accuracy.

A

Counts, proportions, and rates

A proportion is a count divided by a population total; it is dimensionless and bounded between 0 and 1. A ratio compares two quantities that need not be part of a whole. A rate adds a time dimension — it measures how fast events accumulate per unit of person-time.

Why the distinction matters

  • Proportions answer "what fraction is affected right now."
  • Rates answer "how quickly are new cases occurring."
  • Confusing a rate with a proportion is a common source of misinterpreted epidemiologic claims.
B

Incidence vs. prevalence

Cumulative incidence is the proportion of an at-risk population that develops disease over a fixed period. Incidence rate divides new cases by total person-time at risk, accommodating varying follow-up lengths. Prevalence is the proportion of a population that has the disease at a point (or period) in time, regardless of when it started.

$$\text{Prevalence} \approx \text{Incidence rate} \times \text{Average duration of disease}$$
Key relationship: this approximation holds when prevalence is low and disease frequency is roughly stable over time (steady state).
C

Measures of association

These compare disease occurrence between an exposed and an unexposed group.

Risk ratioCumulative incidence, exposed ÷ unexposed. Interpreted directly as relative risk.
Rate ratioIncidence rate, exposed ÷ unexposed. Used with person-time data.
Odds ratioOdds of disease, exposed ÷ unexposed. Approximates the risk ratio when disease is rare.

Absolute vs. relative

Risk difference (exposed risk minus unexposed risk) tells you the absolute burden attributable to exposure; risk ratios and odds ratios tell you the relative strength of association. Both are needed — a large relative risk on a rare outcome may still mean a small absolute difference.

D

Standardization

Crude rates can differ between two populations simply because their age structures differ, not because underlying risk differs. Standardization removes that distortion.

Direct standardization

Apply each population's age-specific rates to a single, shared standard population, then compare the resulting adjusted rates.

Indirect standardization

Apply the standard population's age-specific rates to each study population's age structure to get an expected count, then compare it with the observed count via the standardized morbidity/mortality ratio (SMR).

$$SMR = \frac{\text{Observed cases}}{\text{Expected cases}}$$
When to use which: direct standardization needs age-specific rates for every study population; indirect standardization is preferred when those are unstable (e.g., small strata) but a reliable standard population's rates are available.
E

Screening test accuracy

A screening test is judged against a gold-standard diagnosis using a 2×2 table of test result by true disease status.

SensitivityP(test positive | disease). How well the test catches true cases.
SpecificityP(test negative | no disease). How well the test clears true non-cases.
PPVP(disease | test positive). Depends on disease prevalence.
NPVP(no disease | test negative). Also prevalence-dependent.
Sensitivity/specificity are properties of the test; PPV/NPV are properties of the test in a population. The same test has different predictive values in a high- versus low-prevalence setting.

Common pitfalls

PitfallWhy it happensFix
Treating prevalence as a rateBoth are proportions of "affected," but prevalence carries no time dimension.Reserve "rate" language for incidence measures with person-time denominators.
Comparing crude rates across populationsDifferent age structures create apparent differences unrelated to true risk.Use direct or indirect standardization before comparing.
Quoting PPV as if it were fixedPPV shifts with prevalence even when sensitivity and specificity stay constant.Always state the prevalence a PPV/NPV was computed at.
Interpreting OR as RR in a common outcomeThe rare-disease approximation breaks down once risk exceeds roughly 10%.Report risk ratios directly when using a cohort design and the outcome is common.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • Cumulative incidence is a proportion over a fixed period; incidence rate uses person-time; prevalence is a snapshot.
  • Prevalence ≈ incidence rate × average duration, under steady state.
  • Risk ratio, rate ratio, and odds ratio each compare exposed vs. unexposed, but on different scales.
  • Direct standardization applies each population's rates to a shared standard; indirect compares observed to expected via SMR.
  • Sensitivity/specificity are test properties; PPV/NPV also depend on prevalence.

Formula sheet

$$\text{Prevalence} \approx \text{Incidence rate} \times \text{Duration} \qquad SMR = \frac{\text{Observed}}{\text{Expected}}$$

Likely exam question

A screening test has 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity. Explain why its PPV would be much lower in a low-prevalence population than a high-prevalence one, without changing the test itself.

Answer with: PPV depends on both test accuracy and the prior probability of disease (prevalence); in a low-prevalence group, false positives from the large pool of non-diseased people outnumber true positives even at high specificity.

Mnemonic

SnNout: a highly Sensitive test, when Negative, rules out disease. SpPin: a highly Specific test, when Positive, rules in disease.

Questions to take to class

Use these prompts to turn the guide into an active revision session with your lecturer.

Counts, proportions, and rates
  1. When is a proportion an acceptable substitute for a rate?
  2. How is person-time calculated when follow-up lengths vary widely?
Incidence vs. prevalence
  1. Under what conditions does the prevalence ≈ incidence × duration approximation break down?
  2. How does a sudden treatment breakthrough that shortens disease duration affect prevalence?
Measures of association
  1. Why does the odds ratio diverge from the risk ratio as baseline risk rises?
  2. When is a risk difference more decision-relevant than a risk ratio?
Standardization
  1. What happens if the standard population's age distribution is itself unusual?
  2. Can standardization correct for confounders other than age?
Screening
  1. How would you communicate PPV to a patient in plain language?
  2. What trade-off does raising a test's cutoff create between sensitivity and specificity?
