ANOVA & Categorical Comparisons

Comparing more than two groups, and testing association between categorical variables.

At a glance

The t-test from Week 3 compares two groups. This week extends comparison to three or more groups (ANOVA) and to categorical outcomes (chi-square, Fisher's exact) — the standard tools before moving to regression-based approaches for multiple or continuous predictors.

Compare meansOne-way ANOVA tests whether ≥3 group means differ.
LocatePost-hoc tests find which specific pairs differ.
Compare proportionsChi-square and Fisher's exact test association between categorical variables.
A

One-way ANOVA logic

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) tests $H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2=\dots=\mu_k$ by comparing two sources of variability.

Between-group varianceHow much group means differ from the overall mean.
Within-group varianceHow much individual observations vary around their own group mean.
$$F = \frac{\text{Between-group variance}}{\text{Within-group variance}}$$

A large $F$ means group means differ more than would be expected from within-group noise alone, giving evidence against $H_0$.

B

ANOVA table & assumptions

ANOVA output is conventionally organized into a table partitioning total variability.

SourcedfSum of squaresMean square
Between groups$k-1$SSBMSB = SSB/(k-1)
Within groups$n-k$SSWMSW = SSW/(n-k)
Total$n-1$SST

Assumptions: independence of observations, approximately normal residuals within each group, and equal variance across groups (homogeneity of variance) — the same LINE-style assumptions from Week 4, now applied to a categorical predictor.

C

Post-hoc comparisons

A significant ANOVA F-test only says some groups differ — not which ones. Post-hoc tests make pairwise comparisons while controlling the overall (family-wise) Type I error rate.

Tukey's HSDStandard choice for all pairwise comparisons after a significant ANOVA.
BonferroniDivides α by the number of comparisons; simple but conservative.
Never run unadjusted pairwise t-tests after ANOVA — with $k$ groups there are $\binom{k}{2}$ comparisons, and each carries its own Type I error risk, inflating the overall false-positive rate well above 0.05.
D

Chi-square test of independence

Tests whether two categorical variables are associated, using a contingency table of observed counts.

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

$E_i$ is the count expected under independence (row total × column total ÷ grand total). A large $\chi^2$ indicates observed counts deviate substantially from what independence would predict.

Validity condition: the chi-square approximation is reliable when expected cell counts are at least 5 in most cells. Smaller expected counts call for Fisher's exact test.
E

Fisher's exact test

For small samples or sparse 2×2 tables, Fisher's exact test calculates the exact probability of the observed table (and more extreme tables) under the hypergeometric distribution, rather than relying on the chi-square's large-sample approximation.

When to prefer it

  • Any expected cell count below 5.
  • Small total sample size, even with a larger table.
  • When an exact p-value is preferred over an approximation, regardless of sample size.

Common pitfalls

PitfallWhy it happensFix
Running many unadjusted t-tests instead of ANOVASeems simpler for >2 groups, but inflates Type I error.Use ANOVA first, then an adjusted post-hoc test only if the F-test is significant.
Using chi-square with small expected countsThe large-sample approximation breaks down.Switch to Fisher's exact test when expected counts are low.
Interpreting a significant chi-square as showing causation or effect sizeChi-square tests association, not direction, strength, or causality.Report an effect size (e.g., odds ratio, Week 1) alongside the test.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • ANOVA's F-statistic is between-group variance divided by within-group variance.
  • ANOVA assumptions mirror LINE: independence, approximate normality, equal variance across groups.
  • A significant F-test needs a post-hoc test (e.g., Tukey's HSD) to find which pairs differ.
  • Chi-square compares observed to expected counts under independence; needs expected counts ≥ 5.
  • Fisher's exact test replaces chi-square when expected counts are small.

Formula sheet

$$F = \frac{MSB}{MSW} \qquad \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

Likely exam question

An ANOVA comparing four treatment groups gives p = 0.02. What can you conclude, and what is your next analytical step?

Answer with: at least one group mean differs from the others; run an adjusted post-hoc test (e.g., Tukey's HSD) to identify which specific pairs differ, rather than unadjusted pairwise t-tests.

Mnemonic

ANOVA asks "do any means differ"; post-hoc asks "which ones"; chi-square and Fisher's exact do the same job for categorical, not continuous, outcomes.
