A

Time-to-event data & censoring

Survival data record both an outcome (event occurred or not) and the time until that outcome or the end of observation.

Right censoring occurs when follow-up ends (study ends, participant withdraws, lost to follow-up) before the event is observed — all that is known is the event happened after the censoring time.
Censored observations are not discarded. They still contribute the information "this person survived at least this long," which is why survival methods differ from simply excluding incomplete cases.

Non-informative censoring — the assumption that the reason for censoring is unrelated to the risk of the event — underlies every method in this topic. Informative censoring (e.g., sicker patients dropping out) biases all downstream estimates.

B

The Kaplan-Meier estimator

The Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimator is a non-parametric estimate of the survival function $S(t) = P(\text{event has not occurred by time } t)$, built by multiplying conditional survival probabilities at each observed event time.

$$\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)$$

At each event time $t_i$, $d_i$ is the number of events and $n_i$ is the number still at risk. The result is a step function that drops at each event time and is flat between events (including through censoring times, which reduce $n_i$ without causing a drop).

C

The log-rank test

The log-rank test compares Kaplan-Meier curves between two or more groups, testing $H_0$: the underlying survival functions are identical, by comparing observed vs. expected events at each event time — conceptually similar to the chi-square logic from Week 5, but accumulated across every distinct event time in the data.

The log-rank test does not adjust for covariates — it is a simple group comparison, playing the same role for survival curves that a chi-square test plays for a single contingency table.
D

Cox proportional hazards model

The Cox model relates covariates to the hazard — the instantaneous risk of the event at time $t$, given survival up to that point — without requiring a specific distributional form for survival time itself.

$$h(t \mid X) = h_0(t)\,e^{\beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_k X_k}$$

$h_0(t)$, the baseline hazard, is left unspecified (semi-parametric), while the covariate effects are estimated as hazard ratios, exactly analogous to odds ratios in logistic regression.

$$HR_j = e^{\beta_j}$$

Interpretation: a one-unit increase in $X_j$ multiplies the hazard by $e^{\beta_j}$, holding other covariates constant. $HR=1$: no effect; $HR>1$: increased hazard (worse); $HR<1$: decreased hazard (protective).

E

Checking the proportional hazards assumption

The Cox model assumes the ratio of hazards between any two covariate levels stays constant over time — the survival curves' relative gap doesn't change shape, even if it changes in absolute terms.

Checking it

  • Schoenfeld residuals: should show no systematic trend over time if the assumption holds.
  • Log(-log(survival)) plots: should be roughly parallel across groups.
  • Time-interaction terms: a significant covariate × time interaction signals a violated assumption.
If violated: stratify by the offending covariate, add a time-varying coefficient, or split follow-up into time periods with separate hazard ratios — this is the survival-analysis analogue of Week 6's remedies for a violated MLR assumption.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • Right censoring means the event's timing is only known to be after the censoring time; censored cases still contribute information.
  • Kaplan-Meier estimates $S(t)$ as a product of conditional survival probabilities at each event time.
  • The log-rank test compares KM curves between groups but does not adjust for covariates.
  • The Cox model estimates hazard ratios, $e^{\beta_j}$, without specifying the baseline hazard's shape.
  • The proportional hazards assumption — constant hazard ratio over time — should be checked (e.g., Schoenfeld residuals) before trusting a Cox model.

Formula sheet

Likely exam question

Why can't a simple average of observed survival times be used to summarize a dataset with censoring, and how does the Kaplan-Meier estimator solve this?

Answer with: censored observations' true survival time is unknown, so a plain average would understate survival; KM builds survival probability from conditional "survived this interval" probabilities, using censored cases up to their last known survival time without needing their full survival time.

Mnemonic
