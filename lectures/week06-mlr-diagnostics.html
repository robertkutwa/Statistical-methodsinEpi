At a glance

MLR diagnostics focus on five common problems: non-normal errors, non-linearity, multicollinearity, heteroskedasticity, and outliers, leverage, and influential points.

DetectUse residual plots, formal tests, and diagnostic statistics.
InterpretDistinguish a violated assumption from a harmless unusual observation.
RespondTransform, refit, reweight, remove, or report sensitivity.
A

Non-normal errors Slide 3

Detect with: studentized deleted residuals, a histogram or stem-and-leaf plot, a normal probability plot, and the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Remedies by shape

  • Positive skew: try square-root, log, or negative reciprocal transformations of the outcome; test each for normality.
  • Single or few outliers: check whether they are also influential.
  • Negative skew, large n: the Central Limit Theorem may provide approximate normality for the test statistic.
  • Negative skew, small n: try squared, cubic, exponential, or $\log_{10}(\max(y+1)-y)$ transformations.
B

Non-linearity & polynomial fitting Slides 4–7

Plot studentized deleted residuals against the partial effect of a covariate and smooth the scatter.

  1. If the smoothed line is straight, the relationship is linear—stop.
  2. If it curves, add a mean-centred squared term and refit.
  3. Recheck residuals. If they still curve, add a cubic (third-order) term.
  4. Fourth-order terms are out of scope; orthogonal polynomials are suggested for advanced work.
Interpretation tip: the course focuses on quadratic and cubic polynomials because higher-order terms are difficult to interpret.
C

Multicollinearity Slides 8–14

Multicollinearity occurs when one explanatory variable can be linearly explained by the others. Perfect collinearity ($r=\pm1$) makes $X^TX$ singular, so least-squares estimation fails.

Detection: variance inflation factor

$$VIF_i = \frac{1}{1-R_i^2}$$

Here, $R_i^2$ comes from regressing $x_i$ on all other covariates. A common course rule is VIF > 10, equivalent to $R_i^2 > 0.90$ and approximately $R_i > 0.95$.

Remedies and consequences

  • Remove the offending variable or replace it with a non-collinear proxy.
  • Collect more observations, transform continuous variables, or use ratios/logs.
  • Mean-centre both $x$ and $x^2$ when polynomial predictors are collinear.
  • If ignored: standard errors inflate and coefficient conclusions become unreliable.
D

Heteroskedasticity Slides 15–18

Homoskedasticity means constant variance of $Y$ across predictor levels. Heteroskedasticity violates this assumption—for example, income variance may grow with age.

Detect with: the Breusch–Pagan test. A significant result indicates heteroskedasticity.

Remedies

  1. Fit weighted multiple linear regression (WMLR), using weights inversely proportional to the variance.
  2. Transform $y$ according to how variance scales with its mean:
Variance ∝ mean²Cone-shaped/lognormal spread → log transform.
Variance ∝ meanWedge-shaped spread → square transform.
Variance decreasesWith the mean → reciprocal transform.
E

Outliers, leverage & influence Slides 19–25

OutlierExtreme in y only; has a large residual.
High leverageExtreme in x only.
InfluentialExtreme in both x and y; can distort estimates.

Leverage

$$h_i = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_j-\bar{x})^2}$$

Values lie between 0 and 1. A Hoaglin–Welsch rule flags $h_i > \dfrac{2(p+1)}{n}$, where $p$ is the number of covariates.

Influence

$$D_i = \frac{e_i^2}{p\,s^2}\cdot\frac{h_i}{(1-h_i)^2}$$

Cook’s distance is often flagged at $D_i > 1$. Also consider DFFITS and DFBETAS.

Best practice: report influential points and fit the model both with and without them to assess sensitivity. Do not remove a point automatically.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • Non-normal errors → Shapiro–Wilk and residual plots → transform based on skew and sample size.
  • Non-linearity → smooth partial-effect residuals → add centred quadratic, then cubic terms.
  • Multicollinearity → VIF > 10 is the course red flag → remove, replace, collect data, transform, or centre.
  • Heteroskedasticity → Breusch–Pagan → WMLR or a matched y-transformation.
  • Outlier vs leverage vs influence → use leverage and Cook’s D → refit with and without unusual points.

Formula sheet

$$VIF_i = \frac{1}{1-R_i^2} \qquad h_i = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{\sum (x_j-\bar{x})^2}$$

$$D_i = \frac{e_i^2}{p\,s^2}\cdot\frac{h_i}{(1-h_i)^2}$$

Likely exam question

What is heteroskedasticity? How can it be detected? If found, how can multiple linear regression be fitted?

Answer with: definition → Breusch–Pagan test → WMLR or a variance-stabilizing transformation.

Mnemonic

Outlier = weird Y · Leverage = weird X · Influential = weird X and Y together.

Questions to take to class

Use these prompts to turn the guide into an active revision session with your lecturer.

Non-normal errors
  1. When several transformations look normal, should interpretability or the most normal residuals decide?
  2. How large does n need to be before the Central Limit Theorem is a reasonable justification?
  3. What should be done when the outcome contains zero or negative values?
Non-linearity and polynomials
  1. Is there a formal test for linear versus curvilinear relationships?
  2. How does centring x change interpretation of the squared term?
  3. When several covariates are present, do we check each partial effect separately?
Multicollinearity
  1. Which VIF threshold does the course expect: 10, 5, or both?
  2. Is ridge regression acceptable when theoretically important predictors are collinear?
  3. Should VIF be checked again after adding polynomial or interaction terms?
Heteroskedasticity
  1. If the Breusch–Pagan test and plot disagree, which evidence should guide the decision?
  2. How should coefficients on a transformed outcome be explained to a non-statistical audience?
  3. Can WMLR and an outcome transformation be combined?
Influential observations and assessment
  1. When Cook’s distance and leverage flag one point, when is exclusion justified?
  2. What order should the diagnostics follow, given that one fix can change another diagnostic?
  3. Are derivations expected, or only application and interpretation?
  4. Will we work through one numerical example combining all five diagnostics?
