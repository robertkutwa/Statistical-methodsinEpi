At a glance

Simple linear regression (SLR) models a continuous outcome as a straight-line function of one predictor. Its least-squares fitting method, coefficient interpretation, and assumptions are the direct foundation for multiple linear regression (Week 6) and, with a different link function, logistic regression (Week 7).

FitLeast squares finds the line minimizing squared prediction errors.
InterpretThe slope is the average change in Y per unit change in X.
AssumeLinearity, independence, normal errors, and constant variance — checked in Week 6.
A

The model & least squares

The simple linear regression model expresses $Y$ as a linear function of $X$ plus random error.

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon$$

Least squares chooses $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ to minimize the sum of squared residuals, $\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$, where $\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i$.

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x}$$
B

Interpreting coefficients & R²

$\hat{\beta}_1$ (slope)Average change in $Y$ for a one-unit increase in $X$.
$\hat{\beta}_0$ (intercept)Predicted $Y$ when $X=0$ — only meaningful if $X=0$ is in or near the observed data range.
$R^2$Proportion of variance in $Y$ explained by $X$, from 0 to 1.
$R^2$ is not a measure of whether the model is "correct." A high $R^2$ can coexist with a badly misspecified model (e.g., real curvature fit with a straight line); always check residual plots too.
C

Inference on the slope

Testing $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ (no linear relationship) uses the same t-test logic from Week 3.

$$t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1}{SE(\hat{\beta}_1)}, \qquad df = n-2$$

A confidence interval for $\beta_1$ follows the same $\hat{\beta}_1 \pm t^{*}\cdot SE(\hat{\beta}_1)$ pattern as any other estimate. Degrees of freedom are $n-2$ because two parameters ($\beta_0,\beta_1$) were estimated from the data.

D

Correlation vs. regression

Pearson's correlation coefficient $r$ measures the strength and direction of a linear association, symmetric in $X$ and $Y$. Regression, by contrast, models $Y$ as a function of $X$ and is not symmetric — regressing $Y$ on $X$ gives a different line than regressing $X$ on $Y$.

$$R^2 = r^2 \quad \text{(in simple linear regression only)}$$
Correlation does not imply causation, and neither does regression by itself — a strong, statistically significant slope only supports causal interpretation alongside appropriate study design and control for confounding.
E

Assumptions preview

Ordinary least squares relies on four assumptions about the errors $\varepsilon$, commonly remembered as LINE.

LinearityThe true relationship between $X$ and $Y$ is a straight line.
IndependenceErrors are uncorrelated across observations.
NormalityErrors are normally distributed (mainly matters for small-sample inference).
Equal varianceError variance is constant across the range of $X$ (homoskedasticity).
This is exactly where Week 6 picks up: non-linearity, non-normal errors, heteroskedasticity, and (in multiple regression) multicollinearity are each named and diagnosed there.

Common pitfalls

PitfallWhy it happensFix
Extrapolating beyond the data rangeThe fitted line is only justified where data were observed.Restrict predictions to the observed range of $X$, or flag extrapolation explicitly.
Interpreting $\beta_0$ when $X=0$ is meaninglessMany predictors (e.g., age, blood pressure) never take the value 0 in practice.Center $X$ at a meaningful value before fitting if the intercept needs interpretation.
Assuming high $R^2$ means a valid model$R^2$ only measures linear fit, not whether assumptions hold.Always inspect residual plots (Week 6), not $R^2$ alone.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • Least squares minimizes $\sum(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2$; the slope has a closed-form solution based on covariance and variance of $X$.
  • The slope is a rate of change; the intercept is only meaningful if $X=0$ is plausible.
  • $R^2$ measures explained variance, not correctness of the model form.
  • Testing $\beta_1=0$ uses a t-test with $n-2$ degrees of freedom.
  • LINE — Linearity, Independence, Normality, Equal variance — are the four core assumptions, diagnosed in Week 6.

Formula sheet

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2} \qquad t=\frac{\hat{\beta}_1}{SE(\hat{\beta}_1)} \qquad R^2=r^2$$

Likely exam question

A regression of blood pressure on age gives $R^2=0.62$ and a significant slope. Does this prove age causes higher blood pressure? What else would you want to check?

Answer with: association ≠ causation; check residual plots for LINE violations, consider confounders, and note that observational data alone cannot establish causality regardless of $R^2$.

Mnemonic

LINE: Linearity, Independence, Normality, Equal variance — the four assumptions this course diagnoses one by one in Week 6.
