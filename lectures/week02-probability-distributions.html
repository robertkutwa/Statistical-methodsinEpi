At a glance

Statistical inference rests on probability. This week covers the basic rules of probability, the discrete and continuous distributions that model epidemiologic outcomes, and the Central Limit Theorem — the result that lets us use normal-based methods even when individual data are not normally distributed.

A

Probability rules

Probability quantifies uncertainty on a 0-to-1 scale. Three rules do most of the work in epidemiologic reasoning.

$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$$
$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$$
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)} \quad \text{(Bayes' theorem)}$$

Independence: events $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ — knowing $B$ occurred tells you nothing about $A$. Bayes' theorem is the formal engine behind PPV and NPV from Week 1.

B

Discrete distributions

Two distributions cover most epidemiologic count data.

Binomial

Models the number of "successes" in $n$ independent trials, each with success probability $p$ (e.g., number of diseased individuals in a fixed sample).

$$P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad E[X]=np, \quad Var(X)=np(1-p)$$

Poisson

Models rare, independent events over a fixed interval of time or space (e.g., disease cases per person-year), governed by a single rate parameter $\lambda$.

$$P(X=k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad E[X]=Var(X)=\lambda$$
Rule of thumb: when $n$ is large and $p$ is small, the binomial is well approximated by a Poisson with $\lambda = np$ — this is why incidence counts of rare diseases are typically modeled as Poisson.
C

The normal distribution

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution defined by its mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Converting to a z-score standardizes any normal variable for comparison.

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score expresses how many standard deviations $x$ is from the mean. About 68% of values fall within 1 SD, 95% within roughly 1.96 SD, and 99.7% within 3 SD of the mean — the basis for 95% confidence intervals in Week 3.

D

Sampling distributions

A sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic (e.g., a sample mean) across all possible samples of a given size from a population. Its spread is measured by the standard error, not the standard deviation of individual observations.

$$SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
Standard deviation vs. standard error: SD describes variability between individuals; SE describes variability of a summary statistic (like a mean) across repeated samples, and it shrinks as $n$ grows.
E

Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) states that the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as $n$ grows, regardless of the shape of the underlying population distribution, provided the population has finite variance.

Why it matters

  • It justifies using normal-theory confidence intervals and hypothesis tests even for skewed raw data, once $n$ is reasonably large.
  • It explains why Week 1's rare-event Poisson counts, when averaged over enough person-time, can still be analyzed with normal-based methods.
  • Small samples from skewed populations are where the CLT approximation is weakest — this is exactly the setting where transformations (Week 6) matter most.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A \text{ and } B)$; Bayes' theorem links $P(A\mid B)$ to $P(B\mid A)$.
  • Binomial models a fixed number of trials; Poisson models rare events over time/space, with $E[X]=Var(X)=\lambda$.
  • A z-score standardizes a normal variable: $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$.
  • Standard error is standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{n}$; it describes a statistic, not an individual.
  • The Central Limit Theorem lets sample means be treated as approximately normal for large $n$, regardless of the population's shape.

Formula sheet

$$P(X=k)_{Binomial} = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k} \qquad P(X=k)_{Poisson} = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} \qquad SE(\bar{x})=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Likely exam question

Why can incidence counts of a rare disease be modeled with a Poisson distribution, and why does averaging many such counts still allow normal-based confidence intervals?

Answer with: rare, independent events over person-time → Poisson approximation to the binomial; CLT → sampling distribution of the rate approaches normal as accumulated person-time grows.

Mnemonic

Binomial counts successes in fixed trials; Poisson counts rare events over an interval; the CLT turns "any shape" into "approximately normal," given enough n.
