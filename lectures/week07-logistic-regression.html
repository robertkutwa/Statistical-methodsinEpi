Logistic Regression for Binary Outcomes

Modeling odds, interpreting exponentiated coefficients, and checking model fit.

At a glance

Logistic regression extends the regression framework from Weeks 4 and 6 to binary outcomes (disease/no disease, event/no event). It swaps the linear link for a logit link, produces odds ratios instead of raw slopes, and is fit by maximum likelihood rather than least squares.

LinkThe logit function maps probability (0,1) to the full real line.
InterpretExponentiated coefficients are odds ratios.
FitMaximum likelihood replaces least squares; fit is checked differently than in MLR.
A

Why not linear regression for binary outcomes

Fitting ordinary least squares directly to a 0/1 outcome (the "linear probability model") creates two structural problems.

  • Predicted probabilities can fall below 0 or above 1, which is nonsensical.
  • The variance of a binary outcome depends on its own mean ($p(1-p)$), guaranteeing heteroskedasticity (Week 6) by construction.

Logistic regression solves both by modeling the log-odds of the outcome as linear in the predictors, keeping predicted probabilities bounded between 0 and 1.

$$\text{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_k X_k$$
B

Odds ratios

Exponentiating a logistic coefficient converts it from the log-odds scale to an odds ratio.

$$OR_j = e^{\beta_j}$$

Interpretation: for a one-unit increase in $X_j$, holding other predictors constant, the odds of the outcome multiply by $e^{\beta_j}$. $OR=1$ means no association; $OR>1$ increased odds; $OR<1$ decreased odds.

Recall from Week 1: the odds ratio approximates the risk ratio only when the outcome is rare — for common outcomes, don't casually re-describe an OR as if it were an RR.
C

Estimation: maximum likelihood

Logistic regression coefficients are fit by maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), not least squares: choose the $\beta$ values that make the observed pattern of 0s and 1s most probable under the model.

Unlike OLS, there is no closed-form solution — coefficients are found by iterative numerical optimization (e.g., iteratively reweighted least squares). MLE estimates are asymptotically normal, which is why Wald tests and confidence intervals for logistic coefficients look structurally similar to the t-based ones from Weeks 3-4, but rely on large-sample approximation rather than exact small-sample theory.

D

Assessing model fit

Deviance−2 × log-likelihood; used to compare nested models via likelihood ratio tests.
Hosmer-Lemeshow testCompares observed vs. predicted event rates across risk deciles.
Pseudo-R²Several analogues to OLS $R^2$ (e.g., McFadden's); none is directly comparable across models the way OLS $R^2$ is.
There is no single "$R^2$" for logistic regression — model fit is usually judged with a combination of deviance-based tests, calibration checks, and discrimination measures like the AUC (Week 1's ROC curve idea, applied to the fitted model).
E

Multiple logistic regression, confounding & interaction

With multiple predictors, each exponentiated coefficient is an adjusted odds ratio — holding the other covariates constant. This is how logistic regression controls for confounding: including a confounder in the model adjusts the exposure's odds ratio for its effect.

Interaction

Adding a product term $X_1 \times X_2$ lets the effect of $X_1$ differ across levels of $X_2$ (effect modification). Its exponentiated coefficient is a ratio of odds ratios, not an odds ratio itself, and should be interpreted with that in mind.

Same diagnostics apply: multicollinearity (Week 6) is checked the same way via VIF; predictors should still be assessed for a linear relationship with the logit, not with the raw outcome.

Common pitfalls

PitfallWhy it happensFix
Reading OR as RRBoth express "how much more likely," but only agree for rare outcomes.Report RR directly (from a cohort design) when the outcome is common; state OR explicitly otherwise.
Using OLS diagnostics unchangedResiduals in logistic regression are not continuous/normal like OLS residuals.Use deviance residuals and fit statistics designed for binary outcomes.
Complete/quasi-complete separationA predictor perfectly (or near-perfectly) predicts the outcome, so MLE does not converge.Check for the pattern; consider penalized (e.g., Firth) logistic regression.

Exam prep

Five things to memorize

  • The logit link, $\ln(p/(1-p))$, keeps predicted probabilities between 0 and 1.
  • Exponentiated coefficients are odds ratios: $e^{\beta_j}$.
  • Logistic regression is fit by maximum likelihood, not least squares — no closed-form solution.
  • Model fit uses deviance, Hosmer-Lemeshow, and pseudo-$R^2$ — not OLS $R^2$.
  • Multiple predictors give adjusted odds ratios; interaction terms give ratios of odds ratios.

Formula sheet

$$\text{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0+\beta_1 X_1 \qquad OR = e^{\beta_1}$$

Likely exam question

A logistic regression finds $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.7$ for a binary exposure. Interpret this coefficient, and explain why it is not automatically a good estimate of the risk ratio.

Answer with: $OR = e^{0.7}\approx 2.01$ — the odds of the outcome roughly double with exposure; OR only approximates RR when the outcome is rare, so state the outcome's prevalence before equating the two.

Mnemonic

Logit in, odds ratio out: fit on the log-odds scale, but always translate back to odds ratios (and report prevalence) for interpretation.
